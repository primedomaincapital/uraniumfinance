One Fund Now Holds 81 Million Pounds of Uranium

The Sprott Physical Uranium Trust has become the largest single stockpile of yellowcake outside government hands — and the market cannot decide what to make of it.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 6, 2026 · 7 min read

On November 2, 2026, a trader watching uranium could see two prices for the same pound moving in opposite directions. The net asset value of the Sprott Physical Uranium Trust kept climbing — up for a fifth straight session, to $22.25 a unit, with an implied spot price near $92.74 a pound. The trust's own units, meanwhile, fell 2.58% that day to $18.91, a discount of roughly 15% to the value of the uranium they represent. The same pound, the same day, two verdicts: the physical market says $92.74, the stock market says about $79.65.

That gap is the story of the Sprott Physical Uranium Trust, and the trust is the story of the modern uranium market. SPUT began trading on July 19, 2021, holding 18.2 million pounds of U3O8. By June 30, 2026, it held 81,447,348 pounds, worth $6.93 billion — 98.3% of the trust's $7.04 billion in total net assets, according to Sprott's own second-quarter investor presentation. No private holder of physical uranium comes close. The trust has become, in effect, the world's largest above-ground stockpile of yellowcake outside government hands, and it got there by buying steadily through a market that spent most of 2024 and 2025 going sideways.

The buying pace is what turned a fund into a market force. SPUT bought 3.89 million pounds in 2023 and 3.06 million in 2024, then 8.67 million in 2025 — nearly triple the prior year, according to BMO Capital Markets analyst Helen Amos. In 2026 it accelerated again: about 6 million pounds in the first quarter alone, and roughly 7 million pounds by September 10, the fastest capital-raising pace in its five-year history. The trust operates under an annual acquisition capacity limit of about 9 million pounds, with mechanisms to raise equity beyond that ceiling when demand allows. At its 2026 pace, a single fund was absorbing roughly one pound in every twenty the world's reactors burn in a year.

Put that against the supply arithmetic and the buying looks less like speculation and more like arithmetic. Reactors need about 180 million pounds of uranium a year, while mines produced about 157 million pounds in 2024, according to World Nuclear Association figures. The shortfall — roughly 23 million pounds a year — has to come from stockpiles, and SPUT is the largest stockpile that publishes its holdings every day. The World Nuclear Association's World Nuclear Fuel Report 2025 projects reactor requirements rising from 175 million pounds in 2024 to 391 million pounds by 2040, a 124% increase. A fund that holds 81 million pounds is not a sideshow to that projection. It is a warehouse the whole industry has to price around.

Yet the discount will not go away, and the discount is the market talking back. SPUT's units traded at a discount of about 12.77% on October 30, 2026, and roughly 15% on November 2 — near the record 15.51% set on October 14. A discount that wide says the buyers of the units do not believe the uranium inside is worth what the net asset value claims, or do not believe they will ever be able to realise that value. The trust charges a management fee of 0.35% a year on net asset value, under its January 2026 base shelf prospectus, and it cannot sell its uranium into a rising market without moving the price against itself — the same problem any holder of 81 million pounds faces. A stockpile is easy to build and hard to exit.

The counterargument runs deeper than the discount. A trust that buys uranium is not the same as a reactor that burns it. SPUT's purchases remove pounds from the available market, which supports the price, but they create no new demand for fuel — the pounds sit in licensed storage at conversion, enrichment, or fuel-fabrication facilities, waiting. If the trust's equity raising slows, so does its buying, and the price support goes with it. Kazatomprom, the world's largest producer, mined 67.18 million pounds in 2025 and guides 71.49 to 75.39 million pounds for 2026; Cameco, the second-largest, cut its 2026 production guidance to 19.5–20.5 million pounds after extended maintenance at Key Lake. The physical market is tight because production is constrained, not because a fund is buying. Confusing the two is the easiest mistake in this market.

What the trust really measures is conviction — and the split between its net asset value and its unit price measures how divided that conviction is. The net asset value says the pounds are worth more every month. The unit price says the market will not pay full value for a warehouse it cannot open. For anyone financing uranium, that split is the number to watch: if the discount closes, the trust can raise equity accretively again and keep buying, and the price support continues. If it widens, the buying stops, and the market finds out how much of the last two years' strength was one fund's appetite. The pounds will still be there either way. The question is who values them, and at what price.
