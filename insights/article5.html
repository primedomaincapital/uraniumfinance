The Conversion Bottleneck: Why Mined Uranium Still Has to Wait in Line

The world's reactors need about 60,880 tonnes of conversion a year. The world has about 62,000 tonnes of capacity — and a quarter of it sits in Russia. Mined uranium is piling up behind a three-plant queue in the West.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

A truckload of yellowcake leaves a Saskatchewan mine in the autumn of 2026 worth roughly $91 a pound on the spot market, and it has nowhere to go. Not because nobody wants it — utilities have spent the year signing long-term contracts at $95 a pound and above — but because the next step in the fuel cycle is a queue, not a market. Before uranium can be enriched, it has to be converted into uranium hexafluoride gas, and the Western world has three plants that do it. A miner can dig the ore, mill it, and sell it on paper. Until a conversion slot opens, the pound is inventory, not revenue.

The three plants are easy to name, which is itself the problem. Orano runs the Tricastin complex in France at about 15,000 tonnes of uranium a year, with its Malvési feed plant at roughly 14,500 tonnes and expanding toward 21,000. Cameco runs Port Hope in Ontario at about 12,500 tonnes a year, and its operating licence comes up for renewal in February 2027. ConverDyn runs Metropolis in Illinois at 7,000 tonnes a year — half of its 15,000-tonne nameplate — and it is sold out through 2028 on a term book worth more than $2 billion. ConverDyn is now owned by Solstice Advanced Materials, the Honeywell spin-out, alongside General Atomics, and the partners are studying a second plant, discussed internally as Metropolis 2.0. Three plants, three owners, one queue.

The arithmetic is what makes the queue a bottleneck rather than an inconvenience. World conversion capacity runs about 62,000 tonnes of uranium a year. The operating reactor fleet needs about 60,880 tonnes a year. That is roughly 102 percent utilisation before anything goes wrong — before a maintenance outage, a licence delay, or a shipment held at a border. And a quarter of that capacity sits in Russia: Rosatom's Seversk plant alone holds something like 25 to 30 percent of global conversion capacity. The Prohibiting Russian Uranium Imports Act barred Russian uranium from August 11, 2024 through the end of 2040, with waivers available only until January 1, 2028. American utilities cannot use the Russian share even if they wanted to, which means the effective Western queue is tighter than the global number suggests.

The price has already told this story, for anyone who was watching the right number. Conversion spot prices ran about $64 to $66 a kilogram of uranium in June 2026, with the long-term indicator near $52; by August the spot indicator was $65 and the long-term $55.50. The late-2024 record was $97 a kilogram. In 2017, conversion changed hands for $4 to $5 a kilogram. The long-term price was $50 in 2024 against $34.25 in 2023. A service that cost five dollars a kilogram nine years ago now costs more than ten times that, and the market still cannot buy its way out of the queue, because the queue is made of concrete and licences, not money.

Metropolis is the plant that proves how fast capacity can vanish and how slowly it returns. Honeywell put it on standby in November 2017, when conversion prices were in the single digits and the plant lost money on every kilogram. ConverDyn announced the restart in February 2021, and production only resumed in early 2023 — more than five years from shutdown to first output, for a plant that already existed. That is the timeline a new entrant faces from a standing start, with permitting and construction on top. Nobody builds a conversion plant in a quarter, and the three that exist are the three the West will have for the rest of this decade, plus whatever expansion Orano and ConverDyn decide to fund.

Conversion does not sit alone. It is the first of two front-end chokepoints, and the second is worse. Enrichment — the separative work that turns natural uranium into reactor fuel — ran about $200 a separative work unit on the spot market in August 2026, with the long-term price near $183, against roughly $50 a unit before the 2022 invasion of Ukraine. World enrichment capacity is about 68,076 tonnes of separative work a year: Rosatom holds 29,133, or 42.8 percent; Urenco 17,300, or 25.4 percent; CNNC 13,789, or 20.3 percent; Orano 7,500, or 11 percent. Demand is about 54 million separative work units in 2025 against roughly 64.5 million of capacity. Conversion and enrichment are the same story told twice: the front end of the fuel cycle is where the West's dependence is concentrated, and the mine is the easy part.

The demand side makes the arithmetic worse, not better. The World Nuclear Association puts reactor requirements at 68,920 tonnes of uranium in 2025, rising to just over 150,000 tonnes by 2040 in its reference scenario — more than a doubling in fifteen years, driven by the 78 gigawatts already under construction and the life extensions being granted to existing fleets. Every one of those tonnes has to pass through conversion before it can be enriched, which means conversion demand more than doubles on the same timetable, against a Western plant base that is not doubling. The Red Book puts 2040 production capability from existing, idled, and committed centres at 43,985 tonnes — somewhere between 38 and 51 percent of requirements — and 93,635 tonnes once planned and prospective projects are counted. The mines to meet that demand do not exist yet, and neither do the conversion plants to process what they would produce.

Here is the counterargument, and it deserves a fair hearing. Conversion is a small slice of the total fuel bill — dollars per kilogram against a $91-a-pound uranium price — so a miner's margin is not hostage to it the way a refiner's is. Utilities hold about 170 million pounds of commercial inventories in the United States, a cushion measured in years, not weeks. New capacity is genuinely coming: Orano's Malvési expansion, the Metropolis 2.0 study, Urenco adding 2.5 million separative work units with construction started in August 2026, and Orano's Georges Besse II expansion adding 2.5 million units from 2028. A price that has multiplied thirteen-fold is exactly how a bottleneck gets built out of existence — the signal is working, and the market is answering it.

The catch in that argument is the calendar. A conversion plant takes years and a licence, not a quarter, and the queue is already booked: Metropolis is sold out through 2028, which means a mine that starts producing in 2028 is competing for slots that do not exist yet. Port Hope's licence renewal lands in February 2027, and any delay there removes roughly a sixth of Western capacity overnight. And the utilities' own behaviour — 87 percent of uranium delivered to American reactors in 2025 was bought under long-term contracts, only 13 percent on the spot market — means the front end of the fuel cycle is contracted years ahead, by buyers who learned in 2022 that waiting is the expensive option. The bottleneck will be built out eventually. Eventually is not a date a miner can put in a financing model.

The implication for anyone reading the uranium price is to read a second price alongside it. Spot at $91 says the market is tight today. The conversion price at $65 a kilogram says the tightness is structural, not cyclical — it lives in the step between the mine and the reactor, where three plants serve the whole Western fleet. A mine without a conversion slot is inventory, not revenue, and the three dates that decide whether the bottleneck tightens or breaks are ConverDyn's decision on Metropolis 2.0, Port Hope's licence renewal in February 2027, and Orano's Malvési expansion. The pound in the ground is not the constraint. The pound in line is.
