How Utilities Buy Uranium: The Contracting Cycle That Moves the Price

Utilities worldwide entered 2026 with 3.1 billion pounds of uranium uncovered through 2045 — about 65 percent of what they will need. The contracting cycle that closes that gap, not the spot market, is what sets the price.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

Every morning the uranium spot price prints on a screen, and every morning it misleads somebody. The spot market is where about 13 percent of the uranium delivered to American reactors in 2025 was bought. The other 87 percent arrived under long-term contracts signed years earlier, at prices negotiated in rooms no screen ever sees. The price that matters in uranium is not the one that prints daily. It is the one that gets written into a ten-year contract, and the cycle that writes those contracts is now entering the phase where it moves the whole market.

The scale of what has to be contracted is the first thing to grasp. Utilities worldwide entered 2026 with about 3.1 billion pounds of U3O8 uncovered through 2045 — roughly 65 percent of their projected needs, according to UxC estimates presented by Cameco. American utilities are in the same position in miniature: 52 percent of their maximum anticipated requirements through 2035, or 186.3 million pounds, is still unsecured, against 174 million pounds already committed for 2026 through 2035. The industry spent five years contracting less than it burned. Cameco's own accounting puts the number plainly: utilities contracted 589 million pounds over the five years through 2025 against about 815 million pounds consumed, a replacement gap of 226 million pounds, or 28 percent — roughly 45 million pounds a year of demand that was never locked in.

The reason the gap opened is that contracting is a cycle, not a habit. Utilities buy in waves, and the wave follows the price with a lag of years. When uranium traded in the twenties and thirties, fuel buyers had no reason to sign long deals; the spot market was cheap, inventories were deep, and a procurement manager who locked in a ten-year contract at $50 looked reckless. By the time the long-term price reached $90 in the first quarter of 2026 — its highest since 2008 — the same manager looked reckless for not having signed. The long-term price averaged $55.91 a pound in 2025, according to the Energy Information Administration, against a spot average of $76.01; by June 2026 the long-term indicator was about $94, and in July it printed $95.50 against a spot price of $86.38, a spread of $9.12 that says the future is priced dearer than the present. That inversion is the contracting cycle announcing itself.

Cameco's third-quarter call on October 30 gave the clearest picture of what the cycle looks like from the seller's side. The company's contract book now carries floors in the mid-to-high $70s a pound and ceilings approaching $160, with some fixed-price components in triple digits — terms that would have been fantasy three years ago. Commitments average more than 28 million pounds a year over five years, across 39 customers, with the top five accounting for about 56 percent of commitments. Production guidance narrowed to 19.5 to 20.5 million pounds for the year, against deliveries of 29.5 to 30.5 million pounds: the company is selling more than it mines, drawing down the inventories and secondary supply that have covered the market's shortfall for a decade. The average price American reactor operators actually paid for uranium delivered in 2025 was $58.46 a pound, 11 percent higher than in 2024 — the old contracts rolling off, the new ones rolling on, and the average climbing with every replacement.

The cushion that lets utilities wait is thinner than it looks. American commercial inventories stood at 170 million pounds at the end of 2025, up 9 percent on the year, and mines met 90 percent of world demand in 2024, with the shortfall covered by inventories and secondary supply — a polite way of saying the market eats its savings when the mines fall short. The world's largest holder of physical uranium, Sprott's Physical Uranium Trust, keeps buying, which removes pounds from the available pool rather than adding them. And the supply the world depends on sits in two countries, one of which ships through the other: Kazakhstan produced 23,270 tonnes in 2024, 38.6 percent of world mine output, and Kazatomprom's logistics partly rely on the Russian rail network. Inventories buy time. They do not buy uranium, and the utilities that treat them as a substitute for contracting are spending the cushion, not replacing it.

The demand behind the contracting wave is not a spike. It is a slope. The World Nuclear Association puts reactor requirements at 68,920 tonnes of uranium in 2025, rising to just over 150,000 tonnes by 2040 in its reference scenario — more than a doubling in fifteen years, driven by the 78 gigawatts already under construction and the life extensions being granted to existing fleets. The Red Book, the joint OECD and IAEA survey of uranium resources, puts 2040 production capability from existing, idled, and committed centres at 43,985 tonnes — somewhere between 38 and 51 percent of requirements — and 93,635 tonnes once planned and prospective projects are counted. Read plainly, that means the industry has to build, restart, or permit its way to roughly double its current production capability inside fifteen years, and the contracting cycle is the mechanism that pays for it.

Here is the counterargument, and the utilities make it themselves. The gap is real but it is not an emergency, because utilities are deliberately patient buyers. They hold inventories — 170 million pounds of commercial stocks in the United States at the end of 2025 — and they hold options, in the form of idled mines and uncontracted production that can be switched on when the price justifies it. Contracting is lumpy by design: a utility signs a five-year deal, then goes quiet for three years, then comes back. The 226-million-pound gap is five years of under-contracting, and five years of under-contracting can be closed in two busy contracting seasons if the price stays where it is. The spot market's small share is a feature, not a flaw — it means the system is not hostage to daily noise.

There is truth in that, and it is worth saying plainly, because the bull case for uranium has a habit of treating every number as a crisis. But the counterargument has a hole in it, and the hole is the calendar. A reactor that needs fuel in 2030 needs the contract signed in 2026 or 2027, because the uranium has to be mined, converted, enriched, and fabricated — a chain that takes years, not months, and whose first two steps are already running at full capacity. The 39 customers in Cameco's book are not buying for next quarter. They are buying for the decade, and the top five of them hold more than half the commitments, which means the contracting cycle is concentrated in a small number of decisions by a small number of buyers. When those buyers move together, the price does not rise gradually. It steps.

The implication for anyone watching the uranium price is to watch the contracting announcements instead of the screen. The spot price is the tail. The long-term price is the dog, and the contract volumes are the leash. The numbers to track are the uncovered 3.1 billion pounds through 2045, the 186 million pounds American utilities still need through 2035, and the pace at which Cameco's floors — mid-to-high $70s today — climb toward the ceilings near $160. Every contract signed at a higher floor resets the price every other seller can demand, and every contract signed at all shrinks the uncovered pile. The market does not reprice when a mine opens. It reprices when a utility signs.
