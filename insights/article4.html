The Pound America Doesn't Mine: Wyoming's Quiet Comeback

The long-term uranium price just hit $98 — its highest in 18 years. American mines produced 2.1 million pounds last year. The country burns 47 million.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

The long-term uranium price indicator published by Cameco rose to $98.00 a pound at the end of October 2026, up from $96.50 in August and September, while its spot indicator finished the month at $91.55. TradeTech's long-term assessment sat at $97 a pound from June through September, $13 above the level a year earlier and 13 percent higher year over year — the highest in 18 years, according to Industrial Info Resources. Citi analysts expect the price to rise as high as $125 a pound. On November 9 the spot price printed $92.76. The market has spent the year repricing the future faster than the present: the term price has broken its 2007 ceiling while spot is still below its 2007 peak. The question the price is asking is not whether nuclear is back. It is whether the fuel will be there when the reactors need it.

The American half of that question has an embarrassing answer. U.S. uranium concentrate output totaled 2.1 million pounds in 2025, the most since 2017 and more than triple the 2024 volume — and the country still imports 99 percent of what it uses. U.S. mines account for about 1 percent of world output. Of the 46.9 million pounds delivered to U.S. civilian reactors in 2025, only 7 percent was U.S.-origin, down from 8 percent in 2024. Owners and operators of U.S. reactors paid a weighted-average price of $58.46 a pound for uranium delivered in 2025, 11 percent higher than in 2024, and total U.S. commercial inventories stood at 170 million pounds at the end of 2025, up 9 percent. The United States is the world's largest consumer of uranium — 19,011 tonnes required in 2025, 27.6 percent of world requirements — and it mines almost none of it. The gap is not a rounding error. It is the market.

Wyoming is where the gap is supposed to close, and the numbers there are small, specific, and real. The state has produced more than 238 million pounds of uranium since its first mines opened in 1951, according to the Wyoming State Geological Survey, and most of the country's in-situ recovery production now takes place there. In the second quarter of 2026, U.S. uranium production came from six facilities, four of them ISR operations in Wyoming, which produced a combined 175,249 pounds in the quarter; the largest single contributor to national output was a conventional mill in Utah. Exploration drilling reached 1,824 holes and more than 1.0 million feet in 2025, up from 1,324 holes and 0.6 million feet in 2024. Wyoming accounted for 348 of the 711 full-time person-years employed in the U.S. uranium production industry in 2025. None of those numbers is large. All of them point the same way: the American supply response is a Wyoming story, and it is measured in hundreds of thousands of pounds, not millions.

The reason the response is so small is sitting idle in the same state. The largest licensed ISR plant capacity the EIA lists in Wyoming — 5.5 million pounds a year at Smith Ranch-Highland — belongs to Cameco, whose U.S. operations, Crow Butte and Smith Ranch-Highland, have not produced since the company decided in 2016 to curtail production and defer all wellfield development. A decade later, at $92 a pound, the largest American uranium plant is still dark. Cameco's third-quarter results on October 30 reported net earnings of C$43 million and adjusted EBITDA of C$267 million, and said an outage at the Key Lake mill that began during the quarter resulted in lower production volume and higher costs. The company reconfirmed calendar 2027 production guidance of 500,000 to 600,000 pounds of U3O8 and has no production guidance in place for 2026. The world's second-largest miner is producing below plan, its American plants are idle, and its guidance for next year is half a million pounds. That is not a supply response. It is a press release with a footnote.

The supply the world actually depends on sits further away and closer to the risk. Kazakhstan produced 23,270 tonnes in 2024, 38.6 percent of world mine output, and Kazatomprom, the state company, controls the country's exports; Canada is second at about 11 percent, and Australia and Namibia follow. Kazatomprom's logistics partly rely on the Russian rail network. Rosatom's four enrichment plants — Novouralsk, Zelenogorsk, Seversk, and Angarsk — hold 42.8 percent of world enrichment capacity, and U.S. high-assay low-enriched uranium supply remains heavily dependent on Rosatom. World mine production was 60,213 tonnes in 2024, and reactors required 68,920 tonnes in 2025, with mines meeting 90 percent of world demand in 2024. The shortfall is covered by inventories and secondary supply, which is a polite way of saying the market eats its savings when the mines fall short. The International Energy Agency projects more than 50 percent growth in global nuclear capacity between 2025 and 2050, and 78 gigawatts of new capacity is already under construction. The demand is contracted. The supply is concentrated in two countries, one of which ships through the other.

Here is the counterargument, and the miners make it every quarter. The price signal is working: at $98 long-term, projects that were uneconomic at $50 are economic, exploration drilling is up by a third in holes and two-thirds in footage, and new acquirers are moving into Wyoming's ISR basins precisely because the long-term price justifies it. Cameco's idled plants are an option, not a failure — a company that restarts a 5.5-million-pound plant into a falling market destroys value, and the discipline to wait for the price is what kept the company alive through the decade when uranium traded in the twenties. The physical market's largest holder, Sprott's Physical Uranium Trust, keeps buying, and the utilities that actually burn the fuel have been contracting at the long-term price rather than the spot price — which is why the term price leads. The market is not short of uranium. It is short of uranium that has been mined, converted, and enriched by companies the buyers are allowed to buy from.

That last distinction is the one that matters, and it is the reason the Wyoming story is worth more than its poundage. The United States does not have a uranium shortage. It has a uranium sovereignty shortage: 1 percent mined at home, 7 percent of deliveries U.S.-origin, enrichment dependent on a Russian state company, and the largest domestic plant idle for a decade while the price tripled. A new acquirer in Wyoming does not change the national balance in a year. It changes the option value — the knowledge that the plant, the permits, and the wellfields exist, in a jurisdiction where they can be restarted, if the price or the politics demands it. The market pays for options before it pays for pounds.

The implication for anyone reading the uranium price is to read the whole price. Spot at $92.76 says the market is tight today. Term at $98 says the utilities believe it stays tight. U.S. production at 2.1 million pounds says the domestic response is real and tiny. Smith Ranch-Highland at 5.5 million pounds of idle capacity says the response could be ten times larger without a new mine being built. And Rosatom at 42.8 percent of enrichment says the bottleneck is not the mine at all. The pound America does not mine is not the problem. The pound America cannot enrich, convert, or deliver without asking a rival is. Wyoming's quiet comeback is worth watching — not because it closes the gap, but because it is the only place the gap can be closed from.
