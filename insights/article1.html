The Term Price Just Broke Its 2007 Ceiling — and Spot Is Chasing It

For years the price utilities agreed to pay for future uranium sat below the price of a pound today. That order has reversed.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 6, 2026 · 7 min read

A fuel buyer at a US utility sits down in the autumn of 2026 with two numbers in front of her. One is the price of a pound of uranium today. The other is the price she would have to agree to for pounds delivered years from now. For most of the last decade, the second number was the smaller one. This autumn, it is the larger one — and not by a little. The long-term contract price has climbed past the level it reached at the top of the last cycle, while the spot price, after two years of drift, has started moving again.

The numbers are specific. Cameco publishes a month-end industry average built from the UxC and TradeTech quotes. On September 30, 2026, that series put the spot price at $89.63 per pound of U3O8 and the long-term price at $96.50. The long-term figure is the highest reading in the series, above the $95.00 ceiling that held from May 2007 to March 2008. Spot was up 8.5% on the year; the term price was up 16.3%. A month later the gap had widened further: TradeTech's spot indicator finished October at $93.25, up $3.50 in the final week of the month, 13% above a year earlier and 13.7% higher since the start of 2026, while its long-term indicator stood at $97. Cameco's own long-term indicator reached $98 at the end of October, up from $96.50 in August and September.

That inversion matters because of who sets each price. Spot is the price of a pound available now, set by a thin market of traders, funds, and utilities topping up. The term price is the price utilities agree in contracts for deliveries years ahead — the price that actually finances mines. When term runs above spot, buyers are paying a premium for certainty about future supply. When it breaks an eighteen-year record, they are saying something about how they see the next decade of supply.

The history sharpens the point. In June 2007 the spot price reached $136 a pound, the highest in Cameco's series. The term price then peaked at $95 and never crossed it — spot ran far ahead of term, and the whole move was a spike. This cycle is the mirror image. Spot opened 2024 at $100.25, slid to $64.23 by March 2025, and spent most of 2026 between $84.18 in May and $94.28 in January. The term price, by contrast, barely moved for a year — between $77.50 and $81.50 from March 2024 to March 2025 — then climbed steadily to $96.50 by August 2026. The market is not spiking. It is repricing the future, pound by pound, contract by contract.

What utilities actually pay still lags far behind both numbers, and that lag is the second half of the story. The US Energy Information Administration's 2025 Uranium Marketing Annual Report, published in July 2026, found that owners and operators of US civilian reactors bought 46.9 million pounds of U3O8 equivalent in 2025 at a weighted-average price of $58.46 a pound — 11% more than the $52.71 they paid in 2024, but still roughly $30 below the term price and $35 below the October spot price. The reason is old contracts: deliveries in 2025 were priced under agreements signed when uranium was far cheaper. Those contracts roll off. Every year, a larger share of what reactors burn has to be bought at something close to today's prices.

That roll-off is where the tension sits. US utilities had about 60% of their 2030 needs under contract and only 9% of their 2033 needs, according to EIA data reported in late 2026. The uncovered years are not far away — 2033 is seven contracting cycles out, and a new mine takes longer than that to build. UxC counted about 42 million pounds of long-term contracting through September 2026, against about 45 million a year earlier. The buying wave the term price seems to promise has not yet arrived in volume. Utilities are paying more for the pounds they do contract, but they are not yet contracting many more pounds.

There is a fair counterargument, and it deserves its weight. The term price is an indicator built from a small number of reported transactions and price assessments, not a traded market with deep liquidity. A handful of deals can move it. Spot, for all its movement in late October, still sits 34% below its 2007 nominal peak of $136, and well below that peak in real terms. And the EIA's own survey shows US utilities cutting their maximum anticipated needs for 2026–2034 by 38.1 million pounds, or 10.6%, between its 2024 and 2025 reports — the biggest cut in that window since at least 2017. Some of that may be one large owner revising its plans. But it is a reminder that demand forecasts are not demand, and that the term price can run ahead of the contracting it is supposed to reflect.

The honest reading is narrower than either the bull case or the sceptic's case. The term price breaking its 2007 ceiling is not a prediction that spot will reach $136. It is evidence that the buyers who must secure fuel for the 2030s are now willing to pay more for that security than any buyer has paid since the last cycle — and that they are doing it while spot is still well below its old peak. If the contracting wave follows, the mines that can deliver in the 2030s will be financed on the back of these prices. If it does not, the term price will be remembered as the market's most expensive false alarm. Either way, the next twelve months of contracting volumes, not the next twelve months of price quotes, will settle the argument.
